15.010 · Class 13 · Pricing game

How the game computes demand, profits and prices

What the server does with two prices in each of the three games, with the worked examples from class and a calculator. Describes the code as deployed on 2 November 2026.

1. The market

Every market has 100 customers spread evenly along a street from 0 to 100, one customer per block. Each customer values a drink at V = $200 and pays a walking cost of t = $1 per block to reach a seller. There are two sellers, A at position x1 and B at position x2; both have zero cost per drink, so profit = price × number of customers served.

A customer at block x buys from the seller that leaves the most money in their pocket, and buys nothing if neither leaves anything:

surplus from A = V − pA − t·|x − x1|     surplus from B = V − pB − t·|x − x2|
buy from the seller with the larger surplus, if that surplus is ≥ 0; otherwise buy nothing

So a seller's demand is a stretch of the street. Its reach is how far a customer can be and still get non-negative surplus: (V − p) / t blocks on either side. Where both sellers' stretches overlap, the indifferent customer splits them:

x* = (x1 + x2) / 2 + (pB − pA) / (2t)

A serves the customers from its left reach up to x*, B from x* up to its right reach, both clipped to the street [0, 100]. Market share is demand divided by 100, and the two shares need not add to 100%: the customers in the middle who are too far from both sellers at the posted prices buy nothing.

The examples used in class (sellers at 0 and 100)

A posts $100, B posts $200. The customer right next to B compares $200 (no walk) with $100 + $100 walking: a tie, and everyone to the left prefers A. A serves all 100 customers; B serves nobody. A's profit $10,000.
Both post $175. Each reach is (200 − 175)/1 = 25 blocks. A serves blocks 0 to 25, B serves 75 to 100, the 50 customers in the middle buy nothing. Shares 25% and 25%; profits $4,375 each.
Both post $250. Above what any customer is willing to pay: nobody buys, profits $0.
Both post $100. Reach 100 blocks each, x* = 50: 50 customers each, $5,000 each.

Up to prices of $165 (where the stretch behind a seller at 35 starts to be clipped by the end of the street) Round 2 therefore produces exactly the same demand and profits as Round 1 for every pair of prices. This was already the case in 2025. A faithful "sellers closer together" model would let a seller who undercuts the rival by more than t × 30 = $30 take the whole street, which has no stable price (prices climb, then collapse); the alternative that keeps a clean answer is to leave the sellers at the ends and lower the walking cost instead (see section 3). Special cases the code handles

  • Same position (x1 = x2) or t = 0: pure price competition. The lower price serves everyone who is willing to buy (price ≤ V); equal prices split the market 50/50; a price above V sells nothing.
  • Positions typed the other way round (x1 > x2): computed with the roles swapped and swapped back, so the result is the same as the mirrored market.
  • A price above V: that seller's reach is negative, so it sells nothing whatever the rival does.
  • Nothing non-finite ever leaves the server: if a demand came out as NaN it would be logged and treated as zero (this is what used to crash the 2025 page when t was 0).

3. Reference prices (not shown on the dashboard)

With sellers at the two ends and the market covered (prices below $150, so every customer buys), A's demand is 50 + (pB − pA) / 2. Maximising pA × demand gives A's best reply pA = 50 + pB / 2; where the two best replies cross, both post $100 and earn $5,000 each: the one-shot Nash price. If the two sellers set one common price to maximise their joint profit, the best they can do is the highest price at which the customer in the middle still buys, V − t × 50 = $150, 50 customers each and $7,500 each: the collusion price. Undercutting from $150 to $149 steals customers one block at a time, so the temptation is real but small; that is the whole point of the repeated game.

4. The round, step by step

  • Students are paired at random when the game starts; the pairs stay for the whole game. With an odd number of students the extra seat is ChatGPT (model gpt-5.4 through MIT's Parley gateway), which gets the same information as a student and decides at the start of each round. Without a key on the service that seat posts random prices.
  • The clock: 60 seconds in round 1, then 50, 40, and 30 from round 4 on (the "different time for each round" setting), with a 10-second break between rounds. Prices are accepted only while the clock runs; the last price a student submits before the clock ends is the one that counts.
  • No price by the end of the clock: the server picks a whole-dollar price at random within the allowed range (0 to 250).
  • When the clock ends the server computes both sellers' demand with the formulas above, profit = price × demand, and sends each student their own result and their rival's price, share and profit. The dashboard gets every pair's numbers.
  • The leaderboard is each student's profit summed over the rounds played. The CSV has one row per student per round: round, name, price, rival's name, rival's price, demand, profit, market share.

5. The other demand model in the form (logit, not used in class)

The instructor form also offers a logit market with a market size M, price sensitivity α and a differentiation parameter σ. Each seller's utility to a customer is 10 − α × price, the outside option has utility 0, and shares are

shareA = e(10 − α pA)/σ / ( e(10 − α pA)/σ + e(10 − α pB)/σ + 1 ),   demandA = M × shareA

A larger σ means customers care less about the price difference (more differentiation). σ is kept at or above 0.01 and the exponentials are computed in a numerically stable way, so no setting can produce a division by zero.
