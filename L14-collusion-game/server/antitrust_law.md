## The setting

Two gas stations on the same road sell the same gasoline and compete on price. Each station is an independent
business. They sent each other the messages below on a private channel while setting their prices round by round.
Judge the messages as if the two stations were real competing firms and the messages were real. That it is a class
game is not a defense.

## The law

**Sherman Act, Section 1 (15 U.S.C. 1).** "Every contract, combination in the form of trust or otherwise, or
conspiracy, in restraint of trade or commerce among the several States, or with foreign nations, is declared to be
illegal."

**Price fixing is illegal per se.** An agreement between competitors to raise, lower, fix, peg or stabilize prices is
illegal in itself, whatever its effect (United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co., 1940, a case about gasoline prices).
It is no defense that the agreed prices were reasonable (United States v. Trenton Potteries, 1927), that the
agreement was informal, spoken rather than written, or only implied, or that it failed or was later abandoned: the
offense is complete when the agreement is made. The U.S. Department of Justice prosecutes price fixing as a crime
(fines up to $100 million for a company and $1 million and 10 years in prison for a person).

**What counts as a price agreement.** Any meeting of the minds on prices or on how hard to compete: a common price, a
price floor or range, a joint increase, a promise not to undercut, taking turns, splitting customers, or a "no price
war" understanding. An agreement can be shown by a proposal plus an acceptance in words ("deal", "ok", "sounds good")
or by a proposal followed by a confirmation. Vague or coded words count when the meaning is clear ("let's both keep it
high", "let's be nice to each other", "no undercutting").

**Inviting a competitor to fix prices is also illegal.** Proposing a price agreement to a competitor, even if the
competitor refuses or ignores it, is an "invitation to collude," which the Federal Trade Commission treats as an unfair
method of competition under Section 5 of the FTC Act (for example, its orders against Valassis Communications in 2006
and U-Haul in 2010). In United States v. American Airlines (1984), an executive's call asking a rival to raise fares
20 percent was held to be attempted monopolization even though the rival refused.

**Telling a competitor your future price.** Privately telling a competitor what price you will charge, or that you
will hold your price if they hold theirs, invites them to coordinate and is treated here as an invitation to collude.
Exchanging current or future price information between competitors can itself violate Section 1 (United States v.
Container Corp. of America, 1969).

**Threats.** Threatening to punish a competitor for undercutting a proposed or agreed price ("if you go below $5 I'll
drop to $3.30") is part of enforcing a price agreement and is illegal with it.

**What is legal.** Setting your own price independently, even if it ends up equal to or follows your rival's
(conscious parallelism without an agreement is legal: Theatre Enterprises v. Paramount, 1954; Bell Atlantic v.
Twombly, 2007). Greetings, small talk and jokes that are not about prices or how to compete. Talking about how the
game works or about the class. Commenting on past results that both stations can already see. Refusing a proposal to
fix prices (the refusal is legal; the proposal is not).

## How to decide (two verdicts only)

- **Illegal** if any message, from either station, is a price agreement, an invitation or proposal to coordinate prices
  or not to compete, a private statement of the sender's future price to the rival, or a threat tied to prices.
- **Legal** otherwise, including when there were no messages.

When unsure, ask: would a reasonable jury reading these messages conclude that a station tried to get its rival to
coordinate prices? If yes, Illegal.
